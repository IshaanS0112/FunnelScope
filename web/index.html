FunnelScope

Where users drop out of a GA4 ecommerce funnel, what that is worth, and what to fix first — with the correctness gates that let the numbers be trusted.

The funnel

An ordered funnel: each stage must occur after the previous one, and each stage re-asserts every earlier condition. An unordered funnel — "did this user ever do all four things" — counts someone who bought on Monday and browsed more items on Friday as a completed journey, and inflates conversion for free.

User-scoped vs session-scoped

Correctness gate — the funnel computed twice, by unrelated methods

Method A takes each user's first-occurrence timestamps and compares them with cumulative predicates. Method B collapses those first occurrences into an ordered string (V, C, K, P) and matches a sequence regex. The funnel logic is independent, so a stage predicate that is not cumulative cannot survive the comparison.

The pipeline refuses to emit metrics if they diverge by more than 0.5%, so this page cannot exist for a funnel that failed validation.

This gate fired on the first real export, by 17%, and it was right to. Neither query had a bug — they were answering different questions, and the synthetic fixture could not reveal it because every simulated user did each step once at a distinct timestamp. Two causes, both specific to real GA4 data:

Repeat events. A regex over every event asks "did this user ever cart after ever viewing"; the funnel asks "did the first cart follow the first view". Users repeat constantly here — 6.3 view_item events per user and 4.67 add_to_cart per carting user — and one pattern alone (carted, browsed more, carted again) accounted for 938 extra users.

Batched timestamps. GA4 stamps every event in one network request with the same event_timestamp, so carting from a product listing ties exactly with the view. Method A's strict > drops those users; STRING_AGG ordered only by time kept about half of them. Method B now orders by first_ts, stage_rank DESC, so a tie reads CV and fails the regex — precisely what cart_ts > view_ts does.

Retention

Weekly acquisition cohorts. Retention is any activity in week N — not a purchase, not a session count.

Blank cells are not zeros. A cohort formed in the last weeks of a fixed window physically cannot show week-8 retention: week 8 falls outside the data. Those cells are right-censored and excluded from every average and curve on this page, rather than plotted as zeros. Reading them as a retention collapse is the most common error in this chart, and it is the trap this project was built to avoid.

Acquisition

A channel with high traffic and low conversion is a landing-page or expectation-mismatch problem — spend less. A channel with low traffic and high conversion is an acquisition-scale opportunity — spend more. They demand opposite responses, and a ranked table hides the difference, so volume and rate stay visible together here.

Channels

Channels below 500 users are folded into (long tail). A channel with 40 users and one purchase has a 2.5% conversion rate whose confidence interval contains almost anything; reporting it as a finding would be noise dressed as signal.

What it is worth

users_recovered(S, x) = min(users_entering_S × x,  users_lost_at_S)
recoverable(S, x)     = users_recovered × P(purchase | completed S) × AOV

The percentage-point lift applies to users entering the stage, because that is the denominator of the conversion rate it changes. The common users_lost × pp form is only (1 − rate) of this figure — 30% short at a 30% step, half at a 50% step — and correcting it means multiplying by 1/(1 − rate), which is 1.43× at a 30% step.

Upper bounds, not forecasts. The model applies the purchase rate of users who did complete a stage to users who did not. Recovered users are by construction the ones who needed recovering; they almost certainly convert worse. These figures rank stages against each other. They do not predict revenue.

The absolute figures move across the sweep; the ranking does not. That stability is the defensible claim, which is why the sweep is published instead of a single number.

What I would do first

Reach is measured — users affected, straight from the funnel. Impact, confidence and effort are judgement. There is no engineering team attached to this analysis and no estimate to draw on. They are declared with the assumption behind each one so a reader who disagrees can change a single number and watch the ranking move. The value of the framework is that the ranking is reproducible from stated inputs, not that the scores are right.

How this was built

The memo is the deliverable; this site supports it. Everything below is the short version — docs/methodology.md in the repository is the long one.

Pipeline

   │  scripts/run_queries.py
   ▼
data/processed/*.csv         committed  ──►  _manifest.json records provenance
   │  scripts/analyze.py     ── correctness gates run FIRST ──
   ▼
data/processed/metrics.json  every number quoted anywhere
   ├─► scripts/render_memo.py ──► docs/MEMO.md
   ├─► scripts/build_web.py   ──► this site
   └─► app/streamlit_app.py   ──► local dashboard

BigQuery Sandbox expires tables after 60 days and blocks DML, so nothing is persisted server-side — the repository persists results. The consequence is the good part: because this page reads exported data rather than querying BigQuery live, anyone can open it. A live-query dashboard would run for exactly one person and would be dead in 60 days.

Gates that block publication

Monotonicity, on every segment table: each stage must be a subset of the one before it. A violation means the SQL's stage predicates are not cumulative, which is the most common way a funnel query is silently wrong.

Cross-validation: the funnel computed a second time by a structurally unrelated method, tolerance 0.5%.

Scope sanity: session-scoped conversion cannot exceed user-scoped conversion. If it does, one of the two queries is wrong.

Both gates run before any metric is computed and both raise. It is structurally impossible to produce a memo, a dashboard or this page from a funnel that failed validation.

Statistics

Pooled two-proportion z-test for the statistic, unpooled Wald interval for the CI on the difference, two-sided at α = 0.05. Implemented against math.erfc so the runtime has no SciPy dependency, and cross-checked against scipy.stats.norm in the test suite.

Holm–Bonferroni across the comparison family. Running several segment comparisons at α = 0.05 each does not hold the family-wise error rate at 0.05; with four comparisons it is 18.5%. Both raw and adjusted p-values are reported, and the corrected verdict is the one quoted.

Why bother at these sample sizes? With tens of thousands of users almost anything is significant and the test looks like a formality. It stops being one on the small segments — a 600-user channel, a tablet cohort — which is exactly where an unguarded analysis invents findings.

What this data cannot tell you

It records what users did and is silent on why. The largest single threat to any segment finding is that user_pseudo_id is per browser, not per person: someone who browses on a phone and buys on a laptop appears as two users, one of whom never converts — and a returning user on a second device looks new. That distorts every comparison on this page by an unknown amount. Next steps that would settle it: session recordings on the weaker cohort, a cart-abandonment exit survey, and an A/B test on the top recommendation.

This is Google's public obfuscated GA4 sample export — a demo dataset, not a business problem personally uncovered. The analysis is real; the business is not mine. The repository says so on its front page rather than burying it.

What moved since the previous export

scripts/analyze.py diffs each new metrics.json against the previous one. A pipeline that silently produces different numbers on a re-run is the failure this catches: a query edited by hand, a window quietly widened, a channel that vanished because a source stopped firing. A re-export that moves a number you did not expect to move is a broken query until proven otherwise.
